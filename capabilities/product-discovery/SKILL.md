# Product Discovery

## Mission

Reduce product uncertainty before committing to a large build. Prioritize validated problems and learning, not feature volume.

## Prerequisites

- the product context or initial idea
- access to user evidence or a plan to collect it

## Procedure

1. Frame the intended users, context, job-to-be-done, and business or organizational outcome. Mark assumptions instead of presenting personas as facts.
2. Map existing evidence: user interviews, support requests, usage data, sales conversations, competitor behavior, and operational constraints.
3. Identify the highest-risk assumptions about problem severity, frequency, current alternatives, willingness or ability to adopt, and delivery feasibility.
4. Design ethical discovery activities: neutral interview questions, observation tasks, surveys with clear sampling limits, and small experiments. Avoid leading questions.
5. Separate what participants said or did from interpretation. Preserve sample size, recruitment method, dates, and potential selection bias.
6. Group needs into themes and quantify only when the sample and method justify it. Avoid treating a few anecdotes as representative market demand.
7. Generate solution options and compare them on user value, feasibility, differentiation, cost, risk, and reversibility.
8. Define the smallest test that can distinguish between competing hypotheses. Set success and stop criteria before running it.
9. Recommend a next step: continue discovery, prototype, pilot, build a narrow slice, or stop. Explain what new evidence would change the decision.

## Output contract

- User/problem framing and evidence ledger.
- Assumption and risk map.
- Discovery plan or experiment outline.
- Prioritized opportunity and smallest useful test.
- Decision criteria and unresolved questions.

## Boundaries and stop conditions

- Do not invent customer quotes, interviews, usage metrics, or market demand.
- Do not collect sensitive user data without a justified purpose, consent, and safeguards.
- Do not present demographic stereotypes as user research.
- Do not equate sign-ups, clicks, or survey enthusiasm with retained value.

## Completion rule

Finish only when the requested output is delivered, important assumptions and limitations are visible, and the result has been checked against the output contract. Report what was and was not verified.
